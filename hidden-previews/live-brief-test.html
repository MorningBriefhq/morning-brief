TOP NEWS
Chip stocks crater as Broadcom CEO calls AI investors "casino" gamblers
Semiconductor stocks collapsed across Asia after Broadcom's chief executive likened many AI investors to casino gamblers, triggering a broad de-risking in chip names. South Korea's Kospi index tumbled 6.25%, with Samsung Electronics down 7.4% and memory maker SK Hynix off 10%. In Japan, chip equipment maker Tokyo Electron fell 7.15% while SoftBank Group, a major OpenAI investor, dropped 8.5%. Taiwan's exchange lost almost 2% with TSMC falling by a similar amount. On Wall Street, the tech-heavy Nasdaq declined 0.35% to 30,556. The selloff reflects growing anxiety about AI valuations — the IMF's Global Financial Stability Report released this morning explicitly warned that elevated AI company valuations and mounting debt loads pose systemic risks, stating "the consequences of an AI correction could be global." Asian markets face the most direct exposure given their chip-heavy indices. The key question for U.S. trading is whether this is contained Asian contagion or the start of a broader AI de-risking.
IMF warns AI share crash threatens global financial stability
The International Monetary Fund issued its bluntest warning yet on AI concentration risk, stating that a sharp correction in AI shares could ripple across global markets and threaten financial stability. In its twice-yearly Global Financial Stability Report, the IMF flagged "elevated" valuations for AI companies alongside their increasing debt piles as a key systemic vulnerability. The fund noted that global exposure to U.S. AI assets is at historic highs, meaning a U.S.-led selloff would hit Asian markets hardest, with the UK and euro area potentially more insulated — though investors with U.S. holdings would still be affected. The timing amplifies today's market moves, as the IMF warning landed alongside Broadcom's CEO comments and the Asian chip selloff. This gives institutional investors intellectual cover to reduce tech exposure. The report represents a notable escalation in official concern about AI valuations moving from market commentary to systemic risk assessment.
IMF: Global economy "could soon test the limits of resilience"
The IMF's World Economic Outlook warned that the global economy "could soon test the limits of resilience" as emergency oil stockpiles run low and government budgets become overstretched. The fund said global growth had withstood Middle East conflict better than feared over the past six months, with rerouted oil supplies and strategic stock releases helping contain price impacts. However, those emergency stocks are now depleted, and high debt levels leave governments with limited capacity to shield consumers from future price spikes. "Without shoring up supply capacity and replenishing policy buffers," the IMF warned, the next shock will hit harder. Kuwait's announcement that it is producing 2 million barrels per day and stands ready to boost output provides near-term supply reassurance. But the structural message is clear: the shock absorbers that cushioned the past six months are worn out, raising the stakes for any fresh disruption to energy supplies or trade flows.
JPMorgan misses profit expectations as Dimon warns on deficits and inflation
JPMorgan Chase reported quarterly results that missed profit expectations, with CEO Jamie Dimon using the earnings call to warn about "large fiscal deficits" and "sticky inflation." The profit miss sets a cautious tone for bank earnings season, which continues tomorrow with Bank of America, Morgan Stanley, and others reporting. Dimon's macro warnings carry weight given his track record — when the head of America's largest bank flags fiscal and inflation risks, markets listen. The results will be parsed for signals on trading revenue, net interest income trends, and credit quality. With the Fed in wait-and-see mode and the IMF flagging systemic risks, bank commentary on loan demand and credit conditions takes on added significance for assessing economic resilience.
IMF lifts UK growth forecast, urges fiscal discipline ahead of budget
The International Monetary Fund raised its UK growth forecast and praised Britain's fiscal consolidation efforts, with chief economist Silvana Tenreyro saying the UK "stands out" among G7 nations for having a credible deficit-reduction plan. The endorsement comes just over two weeks before Chancellor Healey's first budget, where he faces pressure to announce energy support measures. Tenreyro's message was direct: "stay the course" on repairing public finances. The IMF's backing provides political cover for fiscal restraint and should support gilt markets. However, the domestic debate remains sharp — the Institute for Fiscal Studies separately warned the budget would be a "work of fiction" without clear plans to cover spending commitments. For UK-exposed investors, the IMF's vote of confidence on fiscal credibility is a positive signal, though the tension between consolidation and spending pressures bears watching.
