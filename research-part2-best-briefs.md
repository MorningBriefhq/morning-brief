# What the Best Morning Market Briefs Do Differently
**Research Part 2 of 3 — Morning Brief product research**
**Date:** 2026-10-12

> Guiding principle (from product owner): *"every sentence should earn its place by helping someone make a better decision or understand something that affects their money."*

This document studies six briefing products — what they include, what they deliberately exclude, and what Morning Brief should steal from each. Where a claim is directly observed in a source, it's marked **[sourced]**. Where it's an analyst's generalization from the evidence, it's marked **[inference]**. Quotes are kept minimal per copyright constraints; everything substantive is paraphrased and cited.

---

## 1. LSEG (Refinitiv) Morning News Call — the institutional-grade template

**What it is:** A weekday email (also distributed via Workspace/Eikon, TradingView, PDF) of market-moving news for the trading day, compiled in Bengaluru and powered by Reuters newswire copy.

**Section structure — observed directly in the July 21, 2026 U.S. edition** [sourced — full edition read at https://refini.tv/3dKUyB8]:

1. **TOP NEWS** — 5 stories, 1–2 sentence summary each (GM earnings, Boeing/Airbus EXCLUSIVE, Anthropic $1.5B settlement, JPMorgan financing exclusive, Hasbro forecast raise)
2. **BEFORE THE BELL** — futures direction, overnight Europe/Asia moves, oil, dollar, gold — 2 short paragraphs of positioning context
3. **STOCKS TO WATCH** — named companies, bulleted, ~60–120 words each (9 companies in this edition: AdaptHealth/Cardinal, Archer, Boeing, Capital One, Cracker Barrel, Grab/Sea/SpaceX, GM, Hasbro, JPMorgan)
4. **PREVIEW** — forward-looking event preview (Tesla earnings/cash burn: "expected to report its first quarterly cash burn in over two years")
5. **ANALYSTS' RECOMMENDATION** — firm + action + one-sentence reason (e.g., Jefferies raised BOK Financial target to $170 on stronger loan growth)
6. **ECONOMIC EVENTS** — time + event + prior reading (0815 ADP pulse: Prior 19,750)
7. **COMPANIES REPORTING RESULTS** — company + expected EPS (Genuine Parts: Expected Q2 $2.08)
8. **CORPORATE EVENTS** — earnings call times by company
9. **EX-DIVIDENDS** — company + amount
10. **PICTURE OF THE DAY** — one photo, one-line caption

**Information categories:** macro wire headlines, single-stock catalysts, analyst actions, dated calendar (econ + earnings + calls), dividends, one image.

**What it EXCLUDES** [inference from the edition's contents]: zero opinion or interpretation — no "why it matters" sentence anywhere; no politics unless it moves markets (tariff/truce items qualify); no crypto in this edition; no lifestyle/sports/culture; no recap of yesterday's close beyond what's needed for today's positioning. **It's a pure day-ahead instrument, not a yesterday-recap.**

**Item length:** Headlines with 1–2 sentence summaries (Top News); 60–120 word bullets (Stocks to Watch); single lines (calendar, analyst actions).

**What-happened vs. why-it-matters ratio:** ~95/5. There is almost no explicit interpretation. The curation *is* the judgment — being selected for Top News or Stocks to Watch is the "why it matters." [inference]

**Lesson:** Institutional-grade = ruthless section discipline. Every item has exactly one home, and the three calendar sections (economic events, reporting companies, corporate events) are the product's backbone — a portfolio manager's day-planner.

## 2. Bloomberg "Five Things to Start Your Day" — the brevity model

**What it is:** A free daily newsletter (the general-audience counterpart to Bloomberg's terminal-only Daybreak briefing, which serves ~325,000 subscribers paying $20k+/yr — see Poynter, 2016) [sourced].

**Structure — observed from published editions** [sourced — news.bloomberglaw.com reprints]:
- Opens with "Good morning" + a **1–2 sentence scene-setter** naming the day's dominant themes ("China's upbeat economic data, the Fed's rate outlook and Giorgia Meloni's assurance of Italy's economic health. Here's what people are talking about.")
- **Exactly five items**, each with a punchy 2–3 word headline ("Slow Puncture," "Too Soon," "Surprise, Surprise," "BOE to Hold") followed by **2–3 short paragraphs, ~100–150 words**
- Each item embeds the *nut paragraph* — the Bloomberg house requirement that every story state what's at stake (per *The Bloomberg Way*: stories must be "First, Factual, Fastest, Final" and account for "Future" events; the ideal lead carries theme + quote + details + nut) [sourced — Wharton/knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu]

**Selection criteria** [inference from edition contents]: "what people are talking about" — conversational relevance among market participants. Items span central banks, macro data, corporate distress (WeWork bankruptcy), housing, crypto volatility. The filter is *market-moving or market-shaping*, globally.

**What it EXCLUDES:** no opinion columns (Bloomberg separates Opinion from News); no deep analysis or data tables; no calendar section; no per-stock watchlist. It's a primer, not a workstation.

**Item length:** ~100–150 words per item; total ~500–750 words plus scene-setter. A true 3-minute read.

**What-happened vs. why-it-matters ratio:** ~80/20. The "what" dominates, but every item carries at least one sentence of consequence (the nut) — e.g., a rate-hold story includes what it means for the loosening timeline. [inference]

**Lesson:** The fixed number is the discipline. Five forces the editor to kill darlings daily; the punchy headline is a compression device; the scene-setter gives the reader the day's *theme* before the details.

## 3. The Daily Shot — the data-visualization-led briefing

**What it is:** A daily email newsletter of charts covering global economic and market trends, founded by Lev Borodovsky (SoberLook) and published by the WSJ. [sourced]

**Format doctrine — stated by the publisher itself** [sourced — freelists.org/list/thedailyshot]: *"The newsletter is intended to be succinct, to the point, with no self-promoting nonsense and no long-winded opinions."* The same page states its exclusions outright: *"If you are looking for Armageddon predictions or conspiracy theories, you will be thoroughly disappointed."*

**What it includes:** charts, each with a minimal caption/annotation. The chart IS the item — a paragraph of text is replaced by a time series, distribution, or map.

**What it EXCLUDES:** opinions, forecasts, punditry, narratives. It also excludes email-address harvesting ("E-mail addresses are NEVER sold").

**The lesson for Morning Brief** [inference]: A chart with a one-line takeaway transmits in 10 seconds what takes 150 words to say. The Daily Shot proves that for a financially literate audience, **data density beats word density** — but only with WSJ-grade chart hygiene (clear labels, honest axes, sourced data, annotations that guide interpretation rather than decorate).

**What-happened vs. why-it-matters ratio:** ~90/10. The chart is the "what"; the title/annotation is the "why." The reader does the interpretive work — which is exactly what a confident, tech-native audience prefers. [inference]

## 4. Strategas / Yardeni Research — what institutional strategists lead with

These are paid institutional research notes. The pattern below is drawn from Yardeni Research's public archive (Morning Briefing editions, 2023) and Strategas material via Baird. [sourced — archive.yardeni.com]

**Yardeni Morning Briefing structure** [sourced]:
1. **Executive Summary with a thesis title** — every edition leads with an opinionated, named thesis: "The Economy Is Beige," "Worry List Update," "Shock Absorbers," "China, Consumers & Alzheimer's Breakthroughs." The summary is 200–400 words of argument: what the strategist believes, what would change their mind, what they're watching. It's signed intellectual work (Ed Yardeni, Jackie Doherty).
2. **Curated reading list by theme** — links only, organized under fixed buckets: Markets, Central banks, US, Europe, China, India, Japan, Geopolitics & trade, Industries & companies, Virus, Beyond markets. No summaries; the categorization is the curation.

**Strategas structure** [sourced — bairdwealth.com Market Strategy Weekly with Jason Trennert]: three-bullet theme headlines ("BULL STILL STRONG ǀ RATES IN FOCUS ǀ POLITICS AS AI RISK"), long-form regime reasoning — bull-market checklists, historical analogs (1987, 1999, Japan 1989), scenario work.

**What strategists LEAD with** [inference from multiple editions]: not the news — the *regime question*. "Why is the market holding up despite X?" "What would have to be true for the bullish case to break?" They assume you know what happened; they sell you the frame for what it means.

**What they EXCLUDE:** single-stock chatter (companies appear only under sector/industry framing); calendar minutiae; analyst upgrades/downgrades; anything that doesn't bear on the regime call. No pictures-of-the-day. No neutrality theater — they're paid for a view.

**What-happened vs. why-it-matters ratio:** Yardeni ~50/50; Strategas ~40/60. The "why it matters" is the product. [inference]

**Lesson:** The best strategist notes open with a thesis sentence a reader could argue with. "Executive summary" is a misnomer — it's an *executive opinion*, and that's why people pay.

## 5. Apollo Academy / Torsten Sløk's Daily Spark — the "one chart that matters" pattern

**What it is:** A daily email from Apollo's chief economist: 2–4 charts, each with a short interpretive paragraph. [sourced — apolloacademy.com/the-daily-spark]

**The pattern — observed in published editions** [sourced]:
1. **One disorienting comparison** stated up front ("US long rates are disconnecting from Fed expectations *and* oil prices" — i.e., the usual explanations fail simultaneously).
2. **The chart(s) as proof** — always sourced ("Source: Bloomberg, Apollo Chief Economist"; "Source: ECB, FRB, Haver Analytics").
3. **1–2 sentences of implication** ("This suggests that long rates are rising because of emerging worries about fiscal sustainability").

A recent edition asked whether AI money agents could trigger an "agentic bank run," built on a single chart: 11 fintech deposit products paying 3.3–5.0% versus the FDIC national average of 0.1% on checking. One number reframes the reader's mental model; the chart makes it undeniable. [sourced — cryptodigicurrency.com / primexbt.com coverage of the Sept 2026 note]

**What it EXCLUDES:** news recaps entirely. No calendar, no earnings, no analyst moves, no headlines. It's analysis-only — it assumes the reader already read the news somewhere else.

**What-happened vs. why-it-matters ratio:** ~30/70. Data + implication; minimal "what happened." [inference]

**Lesson:** The unit of value is the *reframe*: find the single comparison that makes the reader see something familiar differently. Two to four of these per day beats twenty headlines. And the source line under every chart is non-negotiable — it's what makes the reframe trustworthy.

## 6. 1440 — the neutrality/breadth model and what it proves about curation

**What it is:** A free daily email (3.4M+ subscribers, founded 2018, Chicago) promising "news without motives, edited to be as unbiased as humanly possible," designed to be read in under five minutes. [sourced — businesswire.com releases]

**Curation model** [sourced — businesswire.com; pocket-or-print-pop.com review]:
- Editors scour 100+ (per company releases: "hundreds") of sources daily, from NYT/WSJ to BBC to niche science/tech outlets.
- Each story = a **brief neutral summary (a few sentences)** plus **links to the original articles from multiple sources/perspectives** — the reader can check competing takes.
- Tone is deliberately neutral; the product's promise is "we tell you what's happening and where you can learn more," not what to think.

**What it proves** [inference]: (a) Neutrality is a *differentiator*, not a weakness — 1440's growth (Inc. 5000, 1,005% three-year revenue growth, readership evenly split across Independents/Republicans/Democrats) shows exhausted news consumers will pay attention for agenda-free curation. (b) **Source transparency builds trust** — linking out to multiple perspectives is the mechanism, not just the claim. (c) The 5-minute ceiling is a forcing function for brevity that readers experience as respect.

**What it EXCLUDES:** opinion, agenda, outrage framing, "manufactured breaking news alerts" (per co-founder Tim Huelskamp). [sourced]

**What-happened vs. why-it-matters ratio:** ~100/0. The "why it matters" is deliberately delegated to the reader. [inference]

**Lesson:** For contested stories, neutrality + linked sources beats editorializing. But note the tension with the product owner's principle: 1440 optimizes for *understanding the world*; Morning Brief optimizes for *understanding what affects your money* — which requires adding the "so what for your portfolio" sentence that 1440 deliberately omits.

## Cross-cutting observations

**Item-length norms across the best briefs** [inference from all six]:
- Headline + 1–2 sentence summary: the atomic unit (LSEG Top News, 1440 stories)
- ~100–150 words: the standard item (Bloomberg Five Things, LSEG Stocks to Watch)
- ~200–400 words: reserved for the single lead thesis only (Yardeni Executive Summary)
- One chart + one-line takeaway: the densest unit (Daily Shot, Apollo)
- Single lines: calendar entries, analyst actions, ex-divs — never sentences where a line will do

**Ratio summary** [inference]:

| Brief | What happened | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| LSEG Morning News Call | ~95 | ~5 (selection = judgment) |
| Bloomberg Five Things | ~80 | ~20 (nut paragraph) |
| The Daily Shot | ~90 | ~10 (chart title) |
| 1440 | ~100 | ~0 (delegated to reader) |
| Yardeni Morning Briefing | ~50 | ~50 (thesis-led) |
| Strategas | ~40 | ~60 (regime framing) |
| Apollo Daily Spark | ~30 | ~70 (data + implication) |

For Morning Brief's audience (self-directed investors who want decision support, not just awareness), the target band is **~60/40** — more "so what" than Bloomberg, less thesis than a paid strategist. [inference]

## (a) The irreducible core — 7 categories present across all high-quality briefs

1. **Today's market setup.** Futures direction, overnight Asia/Europe moves, key rates (10-yr), oil, dollar, gold. (LSEG "Before the Bell"; Bloomberg scene-setter; Yardeni "Daily Markets Overview"; Scotia-style notes lead with it.) Without this, nothing else has context.
2. **A ruthlessly selected top-story set (≤5).** Every brief selects; selection IS the editorial product. (LSEG Top News; Bloomberg Five Things; 1440's lead stories.) More than ~5 and it's a feed, not a brief.
3. **Stock-specific catalysts.** Named companies with news that could move the stock today — deals, earnings, guidance, regulatory. (LSEG "Stocks to Watch"; Yardeni "Industries & companies"; Bloomberg items are frequently company-led.) This is Morning Brief's "Your Stocks" section — the category is universal; personalization is the differentiator.
4. **The day-ahead calendar.** Economic data (time + consensus + prior), earnings reporting today, notable events/calls. (LSEG runs *three* calendar sections; strategists orient around data releases; Bloomberg's "Future" F.) A morning brief is a day-ahead instrument — the calendar is its spine.
5. **Analyst/sentiment signal.** Upgrades, downgrades, initiations, price-target changes — with the one-sentence reason. (LSEG "Analysts' Recommendation"; strategists track positioning/sentiment; Yardeni tracks breadth.) Tells the reader what the smart money is rethinking.
6. **One visual anchor.** A chart with a point, or a photo that earns its place. (Daily Shot and Apollo are built on this; LSEG closes with Picture of the Day; Bloomberg uses graphics heavily.) One strong visual beats a paragraph.
7. **The "so what" sentence.** At least one line per major item (or a lead thesis paragraph) stating the consequence for money. (Bloomberg's nut paragraph; Yardeni's executive summary; Apollo's implication sentences; Strategas' "what the market is telling us.") This is the single biggest gap between mediocre and excellent — see exclusions below.

## (b) The deliberate-exclusions list — 5 things mediocre briefs include that the best cut

1. **Opinion and punditry disguised as news.** The Daily Shot: "no long-winded opinions." 1440: no agenda, no outrage. LSEG: zero commentary in 10 sections. Mediocre briefs pad with hot takes and talking-head quotes; the best separate news from view — and when they include a view (Yardeni, Strategas), it's *labeled* as the strategist's thesis, not smuggled into the news.
2. **The second-tier story dump.** 1440 proves curation beats comprehensiveness; Bloomberg picks five and kills the rest. Mediocre briefs list 25 links "in case you're interested" — that's a search engine, not a briefing. If it doesn't affect money today, it doesn't make the cut.
3. **Yesterday's recap as the lead.** Every top brief is a *day-ahead* instrument (LSEG literally names it "Before the Bell"; strategists ask "what matters today"). Mediocre briefs lead with "markets closed up X yesterday" — information with no decision value at 7 AM. Yesterday belongs in one line of context, not the lede.
4. **Unsourced macro narratives and prediction theater.** The Daily Shot's stated exclusion: "Armageddon predictions." Apollo sources every chart; 1440 links every story to originals. Mediocre briefs traffic in doom/bubble narratives without data. Rule: no claim without a source line; no forecast without the evidence and the kill-condition.
5. **Non-actionable filler and section overlap.** No lifestyle, no sports (unless it moves a stock), no repeated stories across sections. Note for Morning Brief specifically: LSEG keeps Top News (macro/deals) strictly separate from Stocks to Watch (ticker-level) — a story appears in exactly one place. Mediocre briefs repeat the same story in "Top News" and "Your Stocks."

## (c) What Morning Brief should steal from each source

- **From LSEG:** the calendar trifecta — economic events (time + consensus + prior), companies reporting today (with EPS expectations), and corporate events — as the email's spine; plus the strict one-story-one-section discipline so "Top News" and "Your Stocks" never duplicate each other. Steal the structure, but add the "so what" sentence LSEG omits.
- **From Bloomberg Five Things:** the five-item discipline and the 1–2 sentence "Good morning" scene-setter that names the day's theme before any details; every item gets a nut paragraph answering "what's at stake for your money"; punchy 2–3 word headlines as a compression device.
- **From The Daily Shot:** the chart-led item — replace at least one text item per day with a single sourced chart + one-line takeaway; data density beats word density for this audience, with honest axes and a source line as non-negotiable hygiene.
- **From Strategas/Yardeni:** lead the email with a 2–3 sentence thesis ("executive summary") that takes a view on the day — what regime we're in, what would change the call — instead of opening with a recap; assume the reader saw the headlines, sell them the frame.
- **From Apollo/Sløk:** the "one chart that matters" reframe — hunt daily for the single disorienting comparison (a rate, a spread, a valuation gap) that changes how the reader sees something familiar; always with the source printed underneath.
- **From 1440:** neutrality as discipline for contested stories (state the facts, link 2+ perspectives, never tell the reader what to think) and the hard 5-minute read ceiling — brevity experienced as respect for the reader's time.
